\begin{abstract}
Financial question answering is a temporal, multi-obligation retrieval problem rather than a single relevance-ranking problem. We present \finplan, a vendor-neutral planner that parses a question into explicit filing-period obligations, maintains an evidence state, retrieves under entity, filing, period, and point-in-time constraints, searches for counterevidence, and stops only when obligations are closed or the budget is exhausted. The design is motivated by an impossibility result: a relevance score cannot identify the correct evidence when multiple documents lie in the same score fibre. We formalize the planner as a budgeted partially observed control problem and prove a Bellman decomposition, a point-in-time safety property, and a closure--accuracy boundary condition. On a frozen controlled benchmark (20 seeds, 40 cases per world, seven worlds; 5,600 cases), the state planner improves closure from 0.1011 for single-shot retrieval to 0.3655, reduces mean queries from 10 to 8.8721, and improves utility from -0.4072 to -0.3583. Counterevidence raises recall from 0.3154 to 0.6051 in the matched ablation, while point-in-time filtering removes all observed future leakage in the controlled and validation pilots. Audited LOFin and FinGLM experiments show strong file-level closure but do not establish answer accuracy or superiority over a strong period-aware metadata baseline. We therefore make a bounded claim: \finplan is a representation- and process-level contribution whose answer-level and economic value require the preregistered validation route specified in this paper.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} financial question answering; retrieval-augmented generation; temporal validity; evidence closure; adaptive retrieval; auditability.\\

\section{Introduction}
Financial filings encode facts at several granularities: entity, filing type, fiscal year, fiscal period, document availability time, and sometimes a comparison period. A question such as ``compare revenue in FY2023 and FY2024'' therefore contains at least two obligations. A system can retrieve a highly relevant paragraph and still answer incorrectly when the paragraph belongs to the wrong filing, period, or information set. This failure mode is material for finance because a future filing can leak into a historical question and because an unsupported number can be copied into a model or investment memo.

Recent retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems focus on open-domain retrieval and generation \citep{lewis2020rag,mallen2023parametric,jiang2023flare,jeong2024adaptiverag}. Long-context memory and agent work adds temporal representations, planning, and reflective control \citep{memorybank,packer2023memgpt,park2023generative,rasmussen2025zep,maharana2024locomo,wu2024longmemeval}. These lines establish that retrieval, memory, and action can be improved jointly, but they do not provide a financial contract that enumerates every filing-period obligation, validates document coverage, and enforces a point-in-time cutoff before a reader is allowed to answer.

The research question is: \emph{Can explicit obligation-state planning improve the reliability--cost frontier of financial RAG under temporal and budget constraints, and which parts of that claim are identifiable from auditable evidence?} We make four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item We define a point-in-time financial retrieval contract with an obligation set $O(q)$, an evidence state, a closure predicate, and a conservative reader gate.
\item We give a lower-bound construction showing why relevance alone cannot certify evidence closure, and derive a Bellman formulation, a time-safety theorem, and a closure--accuracy boundary.
\item We implement a vendor-neutral planner and report a controlled benchmark, component ablations, budget sensitivity, and audited external pilots with explicit claim boundaries.
\item We provide a reproducibility and acceptance route: answer-level scoring, strong dense/hybrid baselines, held-out firms and periods, reader calibration, and an economic decision task are required before a stable QF-level claim.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work and Research Gap}
Open-domain RAG combines a retriever with a generator, but a top-ranked chunk is not necessarily a complete or temporally admissible answer context \citep{lewis2020rag,mallen2023parametric}. Active retrieval and adaptive-RAG systems condition the number or timing of retrieval calls on question complexity \citep{jiang2023flare,jeong2024adaptiverag}. Agent frameworks such as ReAct and Reflexion demonstrate iterative action and self-correction \citep{yao2023react,shinn2023reflexion}; memory systems add temporal graphs, consolidation, and long-horizon evaluation \citep{packer2023memgpt,rasmussen2025zep,maharana2024locomo,wu2024longmemeval}. Financial QA benchmarks emphasize numerical reasoning and hybrid table--text evidence \citep{chen2021finqa,zhu2021tatqa,islam2023financebench}.

The gap must be stated narrowly because recent work has already closed parts of it. FinRank explicitly separates evidence retrieval from answer correctness and constructs hard negatives across entities and reporting periods \citep{mansouri2026finrank}; Fin-RATE evaluates longitudinal and cross-entity financial analysis \citep{jiang2026finrate}; FinSAgent and HC-RAG use corpus-side structure and typed evidence paths \citep{chi2026finsagent,chen2026hcrag}; FinCARDS casts filing reranking as schema-constrained matching \citep{chen2026fincards}. These papers make the broad claim ``financial RAG ignores provenance'' untenable. Our narrower, testable gap is the absence of a single finite-budget control contract that simultaneously (i) enumerates typed obligations, (ii) enforces an information-set cutoff, (iii) treats counterevidence as an action, (iv) stops only on closure or abstains, and (v) reports a proof-to-answer bridge under a shared reader. POMDP and active-perception theory supplies ingredients for controlled sensing, but does not by itself instantiate this financial evidence contract \citep{krishnamurthy2017pomdp,shi2025active,satsangi2018active}.

This is a gap in an identifiable property, not a claim that no prior system has any of these components. Our hypotheses are therefore deliberately process-level:
\begin{description}[leftmargin=*]
\item[H1 (representation).] Explicit obligations increase document and period closure relative to single-shot and entity-year retrieval under the same budget.
\item[H2 (safety).] Point-in-time filtering eliminates future-document leakage, at the cost of a measurable coverage trade-off when admissible evidence is unavailable.
\item[H3 (efficiency).] Risk-aware stopping improves utility and query efficiency relative to a no-stop planner, without increasing unsupported assertions.
\end{description}
These hypotheses do not assert that closure equals numerical answer accuracy; that bridge is a separate empirical gate.

\section{Problem Formulation}
For a query $q$, the parser returns obligations
\[
 O(q)=\{o_i\}_{i=1}^{m},\qquad o_i=(e_i,f_i,y_i,p_i,d_i),
\]
where $e$ is an entity, $f$ a filing type, $y$ a fiscal year, $p$ a fiscal period, and $d$ an optional comparison direction or statistic. A candidate document $z$ has metadata $(e_z,f_z,y_z,p_z,a_z)$, where $a_z$ is its public availability time. Given a cutoff $c$, admissibility is $a_z\le c$. The planner maintains
\[
 x_t=(O_t,U_t,C_t,n_t,F_t,\mathrm{closed}_t),
\]
where $U_t$ and $C_t$ are unresolved and covered obligations, $n_t$ is query count, and $F_t$ stores future-document diagnostics. Closure is $\mathrm{closed}_t=1$ iff every obligation has at least one time-valid supporting chunk and no high-risk unresolved gap remains.

With query cost $\lambda$, unsupported assertion penalty $\eta$, and leakage penalty $\rho$, the finite-budget objective is
\[
 J(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\mathbf{1}\{\mathrm{closed}_T\}-\lambda n_T-\eta A_T-\rho L_T\right],\quad n_T\le B.
\]
This objective is a reliability--cost frontier, not a return forecast or an investment recommendation.

\section{Theory: Why the State Must Contain Obligations}
\subsection{Relevance insufficiency}
Let $s(z,q)$ be any scalar relevance score and let $\mathcal{F}_r=\{z:s(z,q)=r\}$ be a score fibre.
\begin{proposition}[Score-fibre lower bound]
If a fibre contains two documents with identical scores but different obligation validity, no policy that observes only $s(z,q)$ can guarantee correct evidence selection. Under a uniform prior over the two documents, its error is at least $1/2$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The policy receives the same observation $r$ in both worlds, so it must choose the same action. One action is invalid in one of the two worlds. The uniform-prior Bayes error is therefore at least one half; additional documents only increase the ambiguity.
\end{proof}
The result is elementary but operationally decisive: metadata and obligation identity are not optional reranking features when the score fibre mixes periods or filings. It proves existence of a failure class, not that every existing ranker fails on every corpus.

\begin{theorem}[Multi-obligation closure lower bound]
Consider $m$ independent obligations, with one valid record and $k$ indistinguishable invalid records per obligation, all having the same relevance score. A score-only policy that selects $m$ records without observing obligation metadata has expected closure at most $(1/(k+1))^m$ under a uniform random presentation order.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For each obligation, the valid record is exchangeable with $k$ invalid records in the score observation. The probability that the first selected record for that obligation is valid is at most $1/(k+1)$. Independence of the presentation permutations gives the product bound. Any policy that selects records globally rather than per obligation can only lose information, so the bound remains valid.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Safe completeness requires abstention]
If an obligation has no admissible supporting record with $a_z\le c$, no policy can both return a supported answer for that obligation and guarantee zero future leakage. A policy satisfying both requirements must abstain or report the obligation as unresolved.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Returning an answer without admissible support violates support; using a record with $a_z>c$ violates the cutoff. The two actions exhaust the possibilities, so abstention is necessary.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Counterevidence is necessary for selective soundness]
Suppose two worlds share the same supporting evidence for an obligation, but in one world an admissible limiting record exists and in the other it does not. A policy that never searches counterevidence must issue the same decision in both worlds and therefore overclaim in at least one world.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The observed supporting evidence is identical, so a no-counterevidence policy has identical output. The correct outputs differ (answer versus limitation/abstention), hence one output is wrong; under equal priors the overclaim probability is at least $1/2$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Bellman decomposition}
Let $a_t$ be a retrieval, verification, or abstention action and $x_{t+1}\sim P(\cdot\mid x_t,a_t)$. Define $V_t(x)$ as optimal future utility with $B-n_t$ queries remaining.
\begin{theorem}[Obligation-state dynamic program]
Under Markov evidence updates and additive costs, $V_t$ satisfies
\[
 V_t(x)=\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}(x)}\{r(x,a)+\mathbb{E}[V_{t+1}(X')\mid x,a]\},
\]
with terminal value $V_T(x)=\mathbf{1}\{\mathrm{closed}(x)\}-\eta A(x)-\rho L(x)$. Thus a planner that ignores unresolved obligations is generally not value-preserving.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Condition on the first action. Additivity separates its immediate reward from the optimal continuation value. The Markov assumption makes $x$ sufficient for the continuation distribution. Backward induction over the finite horizon yields the recursion. If two histories have the same relevance score but different $U_t$, their continuation values differ whenever closing an obligation has positive value; a score-only state is therefore not sufficient.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Point-in-time safety and the closure--accuracy boundary}
\begin{theorem}[Cutoff safety]
If every retrieved document satisfies $a_z\le c$ and the reader receives only retrieved chunks, then the planner has zero future-document leakage relative to cutoff $c$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
All reader-visible chunks are admissible by construction. Hence no chunk with $a_z>c$ can enter the context, so the leakage indicator is zero.
\end{proof}
Closure is not sufficient for answer correctness. Let $G$ be the event that all obligations are closed, $S$ the event that the reader is sound on a closed context, and $Y$ the event of a correct answer. Then
\[
 P(Y)\ge P(G)P(S\mid G)-P(\text{unsupported assertion}\mid G).
\]
The inequality follows by subtracting the probability of an unsupported assertion from the probability of a sound, closed context. It identifies the missing experiment: a reader-level soundness and numerical-answer evaluation.

\subsection{Finite research-gap proof experiment}
To make the lower bounds falsifiable rather than rhetorical, we exhaustively enumerate equal-score presentation orders. For one and two obligations, each obligation has one valid pre-cutoff record and one or two confusable invalid records; a second condition adds a high-score future record. The score-only policy selects the first $m$ records, while the obligation-state policy selects one record per typed obligation after applying the cutoff. The script is \texttt{scripts/run\_gap\_proof.py}; its frozen output is \texttt{paper/results/gap\_proof\_small.json}.

\section{FinPlan-RAG Method}
\subsection{Parser and obligation contract}
The deterministic parser maps aliases such as FY, Q1--Q3, annual, half-year, prior-year, and first-$n$ periods to canonical filing-period tuples. Every tuple remains a separate obligation, so a multi-year comparison cannot be satisfied by one document.

\subsection{Risk-aware planning loop}
At iteration $t$, the planner selects the highest-risk unresolved obligation, forms a targeted query $q\oplus o$, applies entity, filing, year, period, and cutoff binding, retrieves candidate evidence, verifies required fields, searches for counterevidence, and updates $x_t$. Sparse BM25, dense embeddings, and hybrid RRF backends are interchangeable interfaces; the current controlled implementation uses the deterministic sparse backend. The risk score combines unresolved status, missing metadata, conflicting evidence, and remaining budget.

The stopping rule is: close and stop when all obligations are supported by time-valid evidence; retrieve the next obligation when budget remains; otherwise abstain. Relevance alone never closes an obligation. The reader gate emits either an answer with citations or a structured abstention with unresolved obligations and diagnostics.

\subsection{Complexity and interface}
For $m$ obligations and budget $B$, planning requires $O(Bm)$ obligation checks plus backend retrieval cost. The JSONL tool \texttt{finplan\_rag\_plan} accepts an OpenAI-compatible function schema and returns the closed flag, evidence ledger, missing obligations, query count, cost, and recommendation. This makes the same policy callable from Codex, Claude Code, or another code agent without binding the scientific claim to a vendor.

\section{Experiments}
\subsection{Protocol and metrics}
The evidence ladder is: protocol validation, controlled mechanism tests, baseline comparison, ablation, external validation, and stress/failure analysis. Metrics are obligation closure, document coverage, exact obligation match, answer accuracy (only when a gold reader is available), wrong-document rate, counterevidence recall, future leakage, queries, utility, cost, and unsupported assertion rate. Confidence intervals use paired scenario-seed bootstrap or the pre-specified $1.96\,s/\sqrt{n}$ estimator; all future answer-level claims require multiplicity correction.

\subsection{Ablations and budget sensitivity}
Removing counterevidence changes recall from .6051 to .3154, raises overclaim from .0132 to .0350, and improves utility to -.2840 by avoiding verification cost. Removing stopping increases queries to 10 and reduces utility to -.3696. Removing time filtering creates future leakage in .5020 of controlled cases. Closure rises from 0 at budgets 1--2 to .0293, .1816, .3655, and .4552 at budgets 4, 6, 10, and 16, respectively; utility is not monotone because additional queries are costly.

\subsection{Audited external pilots and boundaries}
On the public LOFin set ($n=1,572$, budget 4), FinPlan v9 obtains closure .8919, wrong-document rate .3108, and exact obligation match .4637. A strong period-aware metadata-fill baseline ties closure (.895) and the extended audit supports a lower wrong-document rate and multi-document advantage, but no answer accuracy is available. On FinGLM ($n=1,829$; 11,587 annual-report documents), v9 closure is .997813 with wrong-document rate .012575, while generic adaptive closure is .988518 with wrong-document rate .271186; the gold is self-derived and file-level. In a SEC point-in-time holdout (12 lineages), the reference policy closes 10/12 cases versus 7/12 without time filtering, with 0 versus 5 future-leak cases (exact McNemar $p=.0625$). A Chinese mixed-period pilot closes 3/3 frozen cases versus 0/3 for entity-year retrieval; it is exploratory and uses a deterministic field reader. These pilots validate portability and failure modes, not SOTA or answer-level superiority.

\subsection{Failure analysis}
The principal failure modes are missing or delayed filings, ambiguous fiscal calendars, duplicate amendments, metadata errors, and reader mistakes after correct retrieval. The evidence ledger exposes these as unresolved obligations or diagnostics; it cannot repair a missing source. We therefore report abstention rather than silently filling gaps.

\section{What Is Required for a Stable QF-Level Claim}\label{sec:route}
The present manuscript supports a bounded methods claim. A stable acceptance claim requires the following preregistered gates.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item Release a licensed corpus and timestamp manifest with at least two frozen real-finance datasets, held-out firms and periods, and 5,000 or more answerable questions.
\item Compare BM25, dense (e.g., BGE/E5), hybrid RRF, single-shot, fixed decomposition, generic adaptive, HiREC-style, metadata-fill, and FinPlan versions under the same reader, top-$k$, cutoff, and budget.
\item Add ablations for obligation parsing, period binding, counterevidence, stopping, cutoff, reader gate, metadata-fill, shuffled periods, and timestamp noise.
\item Measure numerical answer accuracy, abstention precision, unsupported assertion rate, calibration, citation completeness, wrong-document rate, future leakage, queries, token exposure, and monetary/API cost. Anchor at least 100 cases with a blinded human or independently audited reader.
\item Run cross-lingual and out-of-domain filing tests and translate the result into a financial ratio, forecast, or decision-sensitivity task without claiming investment returns.
\item Pre-register paired bootstrap/McNemar/permutation tests, Holm or FDR correction, seeds, and the unit of inference; publish manifests, hashes, prompts, and failure cases.
\end{enumerate}

Suggested route thresholds (criteria, not observed results) are: answer accuracy at least 3 percentage points above the strongest baseline on two frozen datasets with a 95\% interval excluding zero; closure at least .95 and exact obligation match at least .85; wrong-document rate at most .20; zero future leakage; unsupported assertion rate at most .05; calibration error at most .05 or human agreement $\kappa\ge .60$; no more than 1 percentage-point deterioration on easy strata; and a statistically defensible query/cost Pareto improvement. Every causal ablation should show a meaningful delta with an interval that does not cross zero. If these thresholds are not met, the paper should narrow its claim to representation-aware financial retrieval and abstention.

\section{Reproducibility, Data Availability, and Limitations}
The repository contains the planner, adapter, tests, controlled summaries, budget sensitivity, real-data provenance, hashes, and the complete overview figure. The current external evaluations are audited but heterogeneous: some use self-derived file-level gold, some lack answer labels, and the Chinese pilot is exploratory. The implementation is deterministic where possible, but dense retrieval, readers, licensing, and filing amendments can change outcomes. These limitations are part of the claim boundary, not post-hoc caveats.

\section{Conclusion}
FinPlan-RAG treats financial RAG as point-in-time obligation completion rather than relevance ranking. The theory explains why an obligation state is needed; controlled evidence shows improved closure, counterevidence recall, and query efficiency; external audits establish portability while exposing the absence of answer-level gold. The next scientific step is therefore not a larger diagram or a stronger claim, but a frozen reader-level benchmark, strong dense/hybrid baselines, calibrated human anchors, and an economic decision-sensitivity task. Until those gates are passed, the defensible contribution is an auditable planning contract with conservative abstention.
